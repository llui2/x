\title{Dynamics of representation, learning, and routing in deep networks}
\maketitle

\section{Literature}

A dynamical description of deep learning has developed along several lines that use different state variables and different limiting procedures. Residual networks make depth a discrete state evolution, and neural ordinary differential equations formalize a continuous-depth limit \cite{he2016resnet,chen2018neuralode}. Training introduces a second evolution in parameter space. Exact deep-linear analyses isolate mode-dependent learning times \cite{saxe2014}, the neural tangent kernel describes an infinite-width regime with nearly frozen features \cite{jacot2018}, and mean-field limits instead retain evolving parameter distributions governed by transport or Wasserstein gradient flows \cite{chizat2018}. These theories describe different reductions of the implemented network and should not be identified with one another.

A second line comes from evolutionary dynamics and information geometry. The replicator equation was introduced as a population dynamics for frequency-dependent selection \cite{taylor1978}. On the interior of the probability simplex, its natural geometry is the Shahshahani metric, which is the Fisher information metric for categorical distributions \cite{harper2009}. Natural-gradient methods use the same Fisher geometry in statistical learning \cite{amari1998}. The connection to learning is more specific than a shared analogy: exponential reinforcement or entropic regularization produces replicator dynamics on mixed strategies \cite{mertikopoulos2016}, and coupled replicator equations have been derived from reinforcement-learning agents interacting through a game \cite{sato2003}. These results provide the correct bridge between evolutionary dynamics and learned probability allocations. They do not imply that ordinary Euclidean gradient descent on arbitrary neural-network parameters is itself a replicator dynamics.

Transformers add state-dependent interactions between elements of a sequence. The implemented attention map is built from learned query, key, and value projections followed by softmax normalization \cite{vaswani2017}. Modern Hopfield networks identify a matched attention update with an associative-memory fixed-point map generated by an energy \cite{ramsauer2021}. Energy Transformer constructs a different architecture whose recurrent attention update is explicitly designed to descend a scalar energy \cite{hoover2023}. Mean-field descriptions instead treat tokens as interacting particles and derive continuum transport equations for their empirical distribution \cite{castin2025,rigollet2025}. A recent formulation couples token distributions evolving through depth to parameter distributions evolving through training \cite{herty2026}. The energetic, particle, and parameter-distribution descriptions therefore overlap in object but not in assumptions.

Mixture-of-experts architectures add input-dependent allocation of computation. Sparse gating increases capacity while evaluating only a subset of experts \cite{shazeer2017}; Switch Transformers use top-one routing with auxiliary balancing \cite{fedus2022}; and expert-choice routing imposes capacity from the expert side \cite{zhou2022}. Theory is beginning to address joint router--expert learning rather than treating the two modules separately \cite{liao2026}. At the same time, recent analyses caution against identifying an expert with a single semantic domain. Routing can reflect hidden-state geometry \cite{wang2026}, and observed specialization can be superposed across fine-grained features \cite{lasy2026}. A common dynamical language should therefore describe allocation without assuming in advance what an expert represents.

The aim here is synthesis rather than a new microscopic theory. We keep four objects distinct: representation states, trainable parameters, routing scores, and normalized allocations. The implemented equations are written first, followed by the continuous, mean-field, energetic, or evolutionary descriptions that can be derived from them. Each connection is marked as an exact rewriting, a limiting approximation, a geometric identity, or an interpretation.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Dynamical objects and levels of description}

Let $\mathbf x_i^\ell$ denote the representation of token $i$ at layer $\ell$. We separate ordinary network parameters $\theta$ from router parameters $\phi$, and write $z_{ie}$ for the score of expert $e$ for token $i$. The corresponding normalized allocation is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf a_i
=
\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf z_i),
\qquad
\mathbf a_i\in\Delta^{E-1},
\label{eq:objects-softmax}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta^{E-1}$ is the probability simplex over $E$ experts.

At the implementation level, the network consists of a depth update and a training update,
\begin{align}
\mathbf x^{\ell+1}
&=
\mathcal F_\ell
\left(
\mathbf x^\ell;\theta,\phi
\right),
\label{eq:discrete-depth}
\\
(\theta,\phi,\mathbf s)_{n+1}
&=
\mathcal U
\left(
\theta,\phi,\mathbf s;
B_n
\right),
\label{eq:discrete-training}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf s$ collects optimizer state and $B_n$ is the minibatch. Equation~\eqref{eq:discrete-depth} describes computation through the network, whereas Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-training} describes learning across optimization steps. These are different dynamical directions.

Continuous descriptions introduce a depth variable $\tau$ and a training variable $t$,
\begin{align}
\partial_\tau\mathbf x_i
&=
F_i
\left(
\mathbf x;\theta,\phi
\right),
\label{eq:spine-state}
\\
\dot\theta
&=
G_\theta,
\qquad
\dot\phi
=
G_\phi.
\label{eq:spine-parameters}
\end{align}
The allocation $\mathbf a_i$ need not be introduced as an independent degree of freedom. If $\mathbf z_i(t)$ is differentiable, its induced motion is
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf a}_i
=
\mathbf M(\mathbf a_i)
\dot{\mathbf z}_i,
\qquad
\mathbf M(\mathbf a)
=
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf a)
-
\mathbf a\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}.
\label{eq:softmax-mobility}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:softmax-mobility} is exact. It is the central bridge between parameter learning and simplex dynamics; the evolutionary interpretation depends on the form of $\dot{\mathbf z}_i$.

The same objects can be lifted to distributions when the corresponding limit is justified. The empirical representation measure is $\mu_\tau^N=N^{-1}\sum_i\delta_{\mathbf x_i(\tau)}$, while a wide network can be represented by a parameter measure $\rho_t$. The common language is therefore not one equation but a hierarchy,
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf x,\theta,\phi,\mathbf z,\mathbf a)
\longrightarrow
(\mu,\rho,\mathbf a),
\end{equation}
with the arrow denoting a controlled reduction rather than an identification.

\subsection{The implemented Transformer map}

Consider a sequence of text converted by a tokenizer into integer token identifiers
\begin{equation}
(u_1,\ldots,u_N),
\qquad
u_i\in\{1,\ldots,V\},
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the vocabulary size. Tokenization is a discrete preprocessing map; the neural network starts from the corresponding embedding vectors. Let $\mathbf E\in\mathbb R^{V\times d}$ be the embedding matrix and let $\mathbf p_i\in\mathbb R^d$ denote positional information. The initial representation of token $i$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf x_i^{0}
=
\mathbf E_{u_i}
+
\mathbf p_i.
\label{eq:embedding}
\end{equation}
Thus a sequence is represented inside the network by $N$ vectors $\mathbf x_i^\ell\in\mathbb R^d$, with the superscript $\ell$ denoting the layer.

For one attention head, queries, keys, and values are linear projections of the current representations,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf q_i
=
\mathbf W_Q\mathbf x_i,
\qquad
\mathbf k_i
=
\mathbf W_K\mathbf x_i,
\qquad
\mathbf v_i
=
\mathbf W_V\mathbf x_i.
\label{eq:qkv}
\end{equation}
The query $\mathbf q_i$ specifies what token $i$ uses to score other tokens, the key $\mathbf k_j$ specifies how token $j$ is scored, and the value $\mathbf v_j$ is the vector transmitted if that interaction receives weight. With key dimension $d_k$, the score from token $i$ to token $j$ is
\begin{equation}
s_{ij}
=
\frac{
\mathbf q_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf k_j
}{
\sqrt{d_k}
}
+
m_{ij},
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where $m_{ij}$ is an optional mask. In a causal decoder,
\begin{equation}
m_{ij}
=
\begin{cases}
0, & j\leq i,\\
-\infty, & j>i,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
so a token cannot attend to future positions.

The softmax maps the score vector of token $i$ to the probability simplex,
\begin{equation}
a_{ij}
=
\frac{
\exp(s_{ij})
}{
\sum_{r=1}^{N}\exp(s_{ir})
},
\qquad
a_{ij}\geq0,
\qquad
\sum_j a_{ij}=1.
\label{eq:attention-softmax}
\end{equation}
The output of the head is the weighted average
\begin{equation}
\mathbf y_i
=
\sum_{j=1}^{N}
a_{ij}\mathbf v_j.
\label{eq:attention-output}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:qkv}--\eqref{eq:attention-output} are the basic self-attention computation. The interaction coefficients are not fixed couplings: they are recomputed from the current representations at every layer.

With $H$ heads, each head has its own projections and produces $\mathbf y_i^{(h)}$. Their outputs are concatenated and projected,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MHA}(\mathbf x)_i
=
\mathbf W_O
\begin{pmatrix}
\mathbf y_i^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf y_i^{(H)}
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
A pre-normalization Transformer block can then be written as
\begin{align}
\mathbf x_i'
&=
\mathbf x_i^\ell
+
\operatorname{MHA}
\left(
\operatorname{Norm}(\mathbf x^\ell)
\right)_i,
\label{eq:attention-residual}
\\
\mathbf x_i^{\ell+1}
&=
\mathbf x_i'
+
\operatorname{MLP}
\left(
\operatorname{Norm}(\mathbf x_i')
\right),
\label{eq:mlp-residual}
\end{align}
where the feed-forward sublayer is typically
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MLP}(\mathbf x)
=
\mathbf W_2
\sigma
\left(
\mathbf W_1\mathbf x+\mathbf b_1
\right)
+
\mathbf b_2.
\end{equation}
The exact activation, normalization, positional encoding, and residual arrangement depend on the architecture. Equations~\eqref{eq:attention-residual} and \eqref{eq:mlp-residual} isolate the common residual structure needed below.

For autoregressive language modeling, the final representation at position $i$ is projected to vocabulary logits,
\begin{equation}
r_{ik}
=
\left(
\mathbf W_{\mathrm{out}}\mathbf x_i^L
+
\mathbf b_{\mathrm{out}}
\right)_k.
\end{equation}
The model probability for the next token is another softmax,
\begin{equation}
p_{ik}
=
\frac{
\exp(r_{ik})
}{
\sum_{q=1}^{V}\exp(r_{iq})
}.
\label{eq:output-softmax}
\end{equation}
For target token $y_i$, the local cross-entropy loss is $-\log p_{i y_i}$. The same mathematical map therefore appears both inside attention and at the output, but over different index sets and with different meanings.

\subsection{Parameter learning and its continuous approximation}

Collect all trainable parameters into $\theta$. For a minibatch $B_n$, backpropagation computes
\begin{equation}
\mathbf g_n
=
\nabla_\theta L_{B_n}(\theta_n).
\end{equation}
The simplest update is stochastic gradient descent,
\begin{equation}
\theta_{n+1}
=
\theta_n
-
\eta\mathbf g_n.
\label{eq:sgd}
\end{equation}
This discrete update is the implemented object from which the usual gradient-flow description is obtained. If the step size is small and the stochasticity of minibatching is neglected at the scale of interest, Eq.~\eqref{eq:sgd} gives
\begin{equation}
\dot\theta
=
-
\nabla_\theta L(\theta).
\label{eq:gradient-flow}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:gradient-flow} is therefore a continuous approximation, not a replacement for the optimizer used in training.

Large Transformers are commonly trained with adaptive optimizers such as AdamW. Ignoring bias-correction notation for compactness, its additional state can be written as
\begin{align}
\mathbf m_n
&=
\beta_1\mathbf m_{n-1}
+
(1-\beta_1)\mathbf g_n,
\\
\mathbf v_n
&=
\beta_2\mathbf v_{n-1}
+
(1-\beta_2)\mathbf g_n^2,
\\
\theta_{n+1}
&=
\theta_n
-
\eta
\frac{
\widehat{\mathbf m}_n
}{
\sqrt{\widehat{\mathbf v}_n}+\epsilon
}
-
\eta\lambda\theta_n.
\label{eq:adamw}
\end{align}
The optimizer is then itself a dynamical system with memory variables $\mathbf m_n$ and $\mathbf v_n$. A Euclidean gradient flow such as Eq.~\eqref{eq:gradient-flow} should only be used when the corresponding reduction is justified.

\subsection{Depth as state dynamics}

The residual structure gives a direct route from the implemented network to a continuous state equation. Introduce an explicit layer step $\Delta\tau$ and write
\begin{equation}
\mathbf x_i^{\ell+1}
=
\mathbf x_i^\ell
+
\Delta\tau
F_i
\left(
\mathbf x^\ell;\theta
\right).
\label{eq:residual-step}
\end{equation}
If the layers sample a sufficiently regular vector field as $\Delta\tau\to0$, the continuous-depth limit is
\begin{equation}
\partial_\tau\mathbf x_i
=
F_i
\left(
\mathbf x;\theta
\right).
\label{eq:depth-flow}
\end{equation}
The variable $\tau$ describes depth or inference time. It is distinct from the training time that parametrizes Eq.~\eqref{eq:gradient-flow}.

Attention makes Eq.~\eqref{eq:depth-flow} an interacting system because $F_i$ depends on the complete token set through the coefficients $a_{ij}$. At finite $N$, the empirical token measure is
\begin{equation}
\mu_\tau^N
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\delta_{\mathbf x_i(\tau)}.
\label{eq:empirical-token}
\end{equation}
Under mean-field assumptions, the limit $N\to\infty$ can be described by a transport equation of the form
\begin{equation}
\partial_\tau\mu
+
\nabla_{\mathbf x}\cdot
\left(
\mu\,
\mathbf v[\mu]
\right)
=
0.
\label{eq:token-pde}
\end{equation}
For specific attention variants, this limit and its well-posedness can be derived explicitly \cite{castin2025,rigollet2025}. The finite Transformer and the continuum equation are therefore different mathematical levels: the particle system is the architecture-level object, while Eq.~\eqref{eq:token-pde} is a large-token reduction.

A similar distinction appears in parameter space. In suitable infinite-width or infinite-head limits, a collection of trainable units can be represented by a parameter measure $\rho_t$. Mean-field learning then takes the generic Wasserstein form
\begin{equation}
\partial_t\rho
=
\nabla_\theta\cdot
\left[
\rho
\nabla_\theta
\frac{\delta L}{\delta\rho}
\right].
\label{eq:parameter-pde}
\end{equation}
Recent Transformer theory couples a token distribution evolving through depth to a parameter distribution evolving through training \cite{herty2026}. This makes the separation between representation dynamics and learning dynamics explicit even when both are described by continuum equations.

\subsection{Energy and fixed-point descriptions}

An energy description is available for particular attention-like systems, but not for an arbitrary Transformer block. Modern Hopfield networks provide a useful exact case. Let $\mathbf X=(\boldsymbol\xi_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol\xi_M)$ contain stored patterns and define
\begin{equation}
E(\mathbf x)
=
\frac{1}{2}
\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x
-
\frac{1}{\beta}
\log
\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}
\exp
\left(
\beta
\boldsymbol\xi_\mu^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x
\right).
\label{eq:hopfield-energy}
\end{equation}
Terms independent of $\mathbf x$ have been omitted. Its gradient is
\begin{equation}
\nabla E(\mathbf x)
=
\mathbf x
-
\mathbf X
\operatorname{softmax}
\left(
\beta\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x
\right).
\end{equation}
The associated fixed-point update,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf x^{+}
=
\mathbf X
\operatorname{softmax}
\left(
\beta\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x
\right),
\label{eq:hopfield-update}
\end{equation}
has the same query--key--value algebra as an attention head under the corresponding identification of patterns and projections \cite{ramsauer2021}. Fixed points satisfy $\mathbf x^+=\mathbf x$. This connection supports an energetic interpretation for the specified construction; it does not imply that the complete trained Transformer admits one scalar energy whose gradient generates every layer. Energy Transformer makes this distinction explicit by modifying the architecture so that a recurrent attention block descends a designed energy \cite{hoover2023}. The existence of an energy is therefore a model property to be established, not a generic consequence of using softmax attention.

\subsection{Mixture-of-experts as allocation of computation}

A mixture-of-experts layer replaces one feed-forward map by $E$ expert maps $F_e(\mathbf x;\theta_e)$ together with a router. For a linear router,
\begin{equation}
z_{ie}
=
\mathbf w_e^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x_i
+
b_e
\end{equation}
is the logit assigning token $i$ to expert $e$. Dense routing uses
\begin{equation}
a_{ie}
=
\frac{
\exp(z_{ie})
}{
\sum_{f=1}^{E}\exp(z_{if})
},
\qquad
\sum_e a_{ie}=1,
\label{eq:router-softmax}
\end{equation}
and returns
\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{MoE}}(\mathbf x_i)
=
\sum_{e=1}^{E}
a_{ie}
F_e(\mathbf x_i;\theta_e).
\label{eq:dense-moe}
\end{equation}

Sparse routing first chooses a support
\begin{equation}
S_i
=
\operatorname{TopK}
\left(
z_{i1},\ldots,z_{iE}
\right)
\end{equation}
and then normalizes only over the selected experts,
\begin{equation}
\widetilde a_{ie}
=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{
\exp(z_{ie})
}{
\sum_{f\in S_i}\exp(z_{if})
},
&
e\in S_i,
\\[8pt]
0,
&
e\notin S_i.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:topk}
\end{equation}
The output is obtained from Eq.~\eqref{eq:dense-moe} after replacing $a_{ie}$ by $\widetilde a_{ie}$. The top-$k$ operation is important mathematically because the routing map is smooth while $S_i$ is fixed but changes non-smoothly when two logits exchange order.

\subsection{Simplex geometry and replicator dynamics}

The allocation variable lives on a probability simplex, so its geometry is different from the Euclidean geometry of the logits. For one token, write
\begin{equation}
a_e
=
\frac{
\exp(z_e)
}{
\sum_f\exp(z_f)
}.
\end{equation}
Differentiation gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial a_e}{\partial z_f}
=
a_e
\left(
\delta_{ef}-a_f
\right),
\end{equation}
or, in vector form, the mobility matrix already introduced in Eq.~\eqref{eq:softmax-mobility}. Along any differentiable score trajectory, define
\begin{equation}
f_e
=
\dot z_e,
\qquad
\overline f
=
\sum_f a_f f_f.
\end{equation}
The induced allocation dynamics is then exactly
\begin{equation}
\dot a_e
=
a_e
\left(
f_e-\overline f
\right).
\label{eq:replicator}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:replicator} has the algebraic form of the replicator equation \cite{taylor1978}. At this stage $f_e$ is a score velocity. Calling it fitness or payoff requires an additional interpretation of the process that generates the scores.

The geometric connection is also exact. On the interior of the simplex, tangent vectors satisfy $\sum_e u_e=0$ and the Fisher--Shahshahani metric is
\begin{equation}
g_{\mathbf a}(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)
=
\sum_e
\frac{
u_e v_e
}{
a_e
}.
\label{eq:shahshahani}
\end{equation}
This is the Fisher information geometry of a categorical distribution \cite{harper2009}. The matrix $\mathbf M(\mathbf a)$ is the corresponding inverse metric on the tangent space. If the score velocity is the Euclidean gradient of a scalar potential on the simplex,
\begin{equation}
f_e
=
\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial a_e},
\end{equation}
then Eq.~\eqref{eq:replicator} is the natural-gradient ascent of $\Phi$ with respect to Eq.~\eqref{eq:shahshahani}. This connects evolutionary game dynamics to the natural-gradient construction used in statistical learning \cite{amari1998}. For a general vector field $\mathbf f(\mathbf a)$, however, the replicator equation need not be a gradient flow.

The discrete counterpart is exponential learning. If scores accumulate according to
\begin{equation}
z_e^{n+1}
=
z_e^n
+
\eta f_e^n,
\label{eq:score-update}
\end{equation}
then softmax gives
\begin{equation}
a_e^{n+1}
=
\frac{
a_e^n\exp(\eta f_e^n)
}{
\sum_f
a_f^n\exp(\eta f_f^n)
}.
\label{eq:exponential-update}
\end{equation}
This is the exponential-weights or entropically regularized reinforcement update. Its continuous-time limit is Eq.~\eqref{eq:replicator}, a standard connection in learning in games \cite{mertikopoulos2016}. Related work has derived coupled replicator systems directly from reinforcement-learning agents \cite{sato2003}. These results justify using evolutionary dynamics as a language for learned allocations when the score update has this structure.

The router used in a neural network requires one further step. Let
\begin{equation}
z_{ie}
=
z_e
\left(
\mathbf x_i;\phi
\right).
\end{equation}
Its exact velocity is
\begin{equation}
\dot z_{ie}
=
\nabla_{\mathbf x}z_e
\cdot
\dot{\mathbf x}_i
+
\nabla_\phi z_{ie}
\cdot
\dot\phi.
\label{eq:router-rate}
\end{equation}
For gradient flow of the router parameters, $\dot\phi=-\nabla_\phi L$, the second term can be written
\begin{equation}
\dot z_{ie}
=
-
\sum_{j,f}
K_{ie,jf}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_{jf}}
\label{eq:router-kernel}
\end{equation}
when the representation contribution is held fixed, with
\begin{equation}
K_{ie,jf}
=
\nabla_\phi z_{ie}^{\mathsf T}
\nabla_\phi z_{jf}.
\end{equation}
Thus the effective quantity $f_{ie}=\dot z_{ie}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:replicator} is generally a data-coupled, parameter-dependent field. Ordinary router gradient descent is not equivalent to assigning each expert an independent biological fitness.

This distinction is already visible for a dense MoE output
\begin{equation}
\mathbf y_i
=
\sum_e
a_{ie}\mathbf h_{ie},
\qquad
\mathbf h_{ie}
=
F_e(\mathbf x_i;\theta_e).
\end{equation}
If $\mathbf g_i=\nabla_{\mathbf y_i}L$, define the marginal loss sensitivity
\begin{equation}
c_{ie}
=
\mathbf g_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf h_{ie}.
\end{equation}
The exact derivative with respect to the router logit is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_{ie}}
=
a_{ie}
\left(
c_{ie}
-
\sum_f a_{if}c_{if}
\right).
\label{eq:router-gradient}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:router-gradient} already has a centered relative-performance structure, but Euclidean gradient descent on $z$ sets $\dot z=-\nabla_zL$ and therefore applies the softmax mobility once more when converted to $\dot a$. The standard replicator dynamics arises directly from score accumulation as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:score-update}, not from every possible gradient parameterization of the router.

For top-$k$ routing, the smooth equations hold only while the active set is unchanged. Crossings of router scores change the support and introduce switching events. The natural mathematical object is then a piecewise-smooth or hybrid simplex dynamics rather than a single smooth replicator flow.

\subsection{Specialization and collapse are distinct observables}

Let $X$ denote a token drawn from the data distribution and let $a_e(X)$ be its soft routing weights. Define the average occupation of expert $e$ by
\begin{equation}
q_e
=
\mathbb E_X
\left[
a_e(X)
\right].
\end{equation}
The global load entropy is
\begin{equation}
H(q)
=
-
\sum_e q_e\log q_e,
\end{equation}
while the mean routing entropy is
\begin{equation}
U
=
\mathbb E_X
\left[
-
\sum_e
a_e(X)\log a_e(X)
\right].
\end{equation}
Their difference is
\begin{equation}
S
=
H(q)-U
=
I(X;E),
\label{eq:specialization}
\end{equation}
where $E$ is the random expert selected according to $a(X)$. Equation~\eqref{eq:specialization} is the mutual information between tokens and expert assignments. It measures token-dependent specialization independently of whether the global load is balanced. A complementary collapse measure is
\begin{equation}
C
=
\log E-H(q).
\label{eq:collapse}
\end{equation}
Uniform soft routing has $C=0$ and $S=0$. Balanced deterministic routing has $C=0$ and large $S$. Routing concentrated on one expert has large $C$ and $S=0$. These cases separate load imbalance from specialization without assuming that an expert corresponds to one semantic domain.

\subsection{Relations between the existing theories}

The different theoretical descriptions can now be placed on the same chain without treating them as equivalent:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
relation & status & object \\
\hline
residual layer $\to$ ODE & continuous-depth limit & $\mathbf x$ \\
finite attention $\to$ particles & exact rewriting & $\{\mathbf x_i\}$ \\
particles $\to$ transport PDE & mean-field limit & $\mu$ \\
SGD $\to$ gradient flow & small-step approximation & $\theta,\phi$ \\
wide model $\to$ parameter PDE & mean-field limit & $\rho$ \\
Hopfield update $\leftrightarrow$ attention & exact for matched form & $\mathbf x$ \\
softmax score motion $\to$ replicator form & exact identity & $\mathbf a$ \\
exponential reinforcement $\to$ replicator & continuous-time limit & $\mathbf a$ \\
replicator $\to$ natural gradient & potential case & $\mathbf a$ \\
specialization $\leftrightarrow$ evolutionary niches & interpretation & $\mathbf a,\theta$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The geometries are correspondingly different. Representation dynamics is usually written in the ambient feature space, parameter learning may use Euclidean, Fisher, or Wasserstein geometry depending on the model and limit, and allocations live on a categorical simplex with Fisher--Shahshahani geometry. The common language lies in keeping the state variables and maps explicit while translating between these spaces only through stated reductions.

\subsection{Fixed points and coupled questions}

Each level has its own stationary condition. A representation fixed point satisfies
\begin{equation}
F_i(\mathbf x^*;\theta,\phi)=0,
\end{equation}
while a stationary point of Euclidean gradient flow satisfies
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\theta L(\theta^*,\phi^*)=0,
\qquad
\nabla_\phi L(\theta^*,\phi^*)=0.
\end{equation}
For the induced allocation dynamics in Eq.~\eqref{eq:replicator},
\begin{equation}
a_e^*>0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f_e^*=\overline f^*
\end{equation}
at an interior stationary allocation. Boundary equilibria allow inactive experts with $a_e^*=0$. This is the usual equal-payoff condition on the active support, but in a neural router the effective field $f$ must first be derived from Eq.~\eqref{eq:router-rate} or Eq.~\eqref{eq:router-kernel}.

The coupled problem is therefore not one fixed-point problem but the interaction of several dynamical levels. Relevant control parameters include depth, learning rate, router temperature, expert capacity, sparsity $k$, width, and explicit load-balancing terms. Current MoE theory already shows that router and expert learning can be coupled during feature acquisition \cite{liao2026}, while empirical and mechanistic work indicates that routing structure is strongly constrained by the geometry of hidden representations \cite{wang2026,lasy2026}. These results fit naturally into Eqs.~\eqref{eq:spine-state}, \eqref{eq:spine-parameters}, and \eqref{eq:softmax-mobility}: the representation changes the score landscape, the router changes token allocation, and allocation changes which data train each expert.

A concrete future direction is to derive reduced equations for this feedback loop rather than postulate an evolutionary model independently. Starting from the finite gradient equations, one can ask when the effective score velocity becomes local enough to admit a payoff description, when the induced simplex dynamics has a potential, and when expert symmetry loses stability. In such regimes, evolutionary concepts such as coexistence, exclusion, mutation-like exploration, and evolutionary stability become mathematically testable statements about the reduced neural dynamics rather than analogies.

\section{Discussion}

The implemented Transformer is only the microscopic starting point. The common theory is the relation between its state dynamics, parameter dynamics, and probability-valued interaction or allocation variables. Continuous depth, mean-field token equations, parameter transport equations, energy functions, and evolutionary dynamics each become useful only after specifying the reduction that produces them.

The evolutionary connection is strongest at the simplex level. Softmax induces the Fisher--Shahshahani mobility exactly, exponential score learning produces replicator dynamics, and potential replicator systems are natural-gradient flows. The unresolved step for modern MoE models is not this geometry but the effective score field generated by joint representation, router, and expert learning. Deriving that field from the actual optimization dynamics provides a controlled route from present architectures to future analyses of specialization, symmetry breaking, coexistence, and collapse.
